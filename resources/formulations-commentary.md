# Rules and Criteria for Evaluating Formulations

This file contains rules, formulas, and criteria for evaluating elements of an academic paper.
Examples of formulations are in `formulations-guide.md`.

---

## 1. Research Problem

**Formula:** "X exists / is happening, but Y has not been studied / resolved / contradicts Z."

**Criteria for a correct problem:**
- Contains a specific contradiction, not a statement of the topic's importance
- Points to a gap: what exactly has not been studied, resolved, or explained
- Identifies the consequence of the gap: what the unresolved problem leads to
- Contains specifics: which aspect, which sector, which context

**Typical errors:**
- Substituting the topic for the problem — a domain is named, but there is no contradiction
- Problem is too broad — impossible to resolve within a single paper
- Problem is a platitude — true for any period and context
- No research gap — what has been studied is described, but what is missing is not stated

---

## 2. Relevance

**Three pillars of relevance** (minimum two of three):
1. Data: specific statistics, trends with numbers and sources
2. Institutional demand: strategies, programs, regulatory documents
3. Scientific gap: quantitative characterization of the literature gap (how many papers, which databases, which period)

**Criteria for correct relevance:**
- Based on facts (data, trends, gaps), not on general statements
- Contains both a practical and a scientific component
- Uses current data (no older than 5 years for fast-moving topics)
- Shows why research is needed, not just a practical solution

**Typical errors:**
- Empty formulas: "In today's world...", "At present..."
- Practical relevance only, without scientific justification
- Scientific relevance only, without connection to practice
- References to outdated or irrelevant data

---

## 3. State of Research on the Problem

**Criteria for a correct literature review:**
- Authors are grouped by schools, approaches, or aspects of the problem
- Methodological differences between groups are specified (quantitative vs. qualitative, large vs. small samples)
- The review concludes with a clear identification of the gap — what exactly is not covered
- Domestic and international works are compared substantively, not merely listed separately

**Typical errors:**
- Authors listed sequentially without systematization by approach
- No conclusion about the gap after the review — the review "hangs" without a logical endpoint
- Formal division into "domestic and international" without comparing approaches

---

## 4. Research Goal

**Formula:** [result verb] + [what specifically] + [on what material / in what context].

**Three criteria for a correct goal:**
1. Achievability — can realistically be achieved within this paper
2. Measurability — can objectively determine whether the goal has been achieved
3. Singularity — one goal, not a list separated by commas

**Result verbs** (point to a specific output):
- develop, identify, assess, determine, justify, verify, classify

**Process verbs** (point to a vague goal):
- study, consider, examine, reveal, illuminate

**Typical errors:**
- Process-goal instead of result-goal
- Goal is a list of actions separated by commas (these are objectives)
- Goal is not measurable — no criterion for achievement
- Mismatch between goal and problem
- Vague verbs

---

## 5. Research Objectives

**Principle:** objectives are the decomposition of the goal into sequential steps. Each objective is a verifiable action leading to the goal. The order of objectives = the logic of the study.

**Criteria for correct objectives:**
- Each objective logically follows from the goal
- The set of objectives fully covers the goal (no missing steps)
- The result of each objective is used in subsequent objectives or toward achieving the goal
- Formulated via an action verb with a specific result
- Objectives are arranged in logical sequence: theory → method → data → result → recommendations

**Typical errors:**
- Irrelevant objective — its result is not used toward achieving the goal
- Objectives do not cover the goal — a key step is missing
- Objective = retelling of a chapter — describes a process, not a result
- Vague verbs: "consider", "study", "reveal"
- Logical sequence violated

---

## 6. Hypothesis

**Formula:** "If [condition/factor], then [consequence], because [mechanism]."

**Four criteria for a correct hypothesis:**
1. Falsifiability — can be refuted empirically
2. Testability — data and methods exist to test it
3. Specificity — contains variables and the assumed relationship between them
4. Non-triviality — not an obvious statement

**Typical errors:**
- Truism hypothesis — obvious and requires no research
- Non-falsifiable hypothesis — cannot be refuted
- Hypothesis as disguised goal — an untestable expectation
- Hypothesis without variables — no specification of concrete quantities and relationships

**Note:** if the study is purely empirical and a hypothesis is absent — this is a problem. If the study is descriptive or exploratory — the hypothesis may be replaced by research questions, but this must be justified.

---

## 7. Object and Subject

**Principle:** the object is the area of reality under study; the subject is the specific aspect of the object being studied. The subject is always narrower than the object.

**Criteria:**
- Object is broader than subject (object = domain, subject = specific aspect)
- Subject is aligned with the research goal
- Object is proportionate to the scale of the study (not too broad for the sample, not too narrow for generalization)

**Typical errors:**
- Object = subject (same thing rephrased)
- Subject is not aligned with the goal
- Object is too broad (when studying a narrow slice) or too narrow (when claiming generalization)

---

## 8. Scientific Novelty

**Principle:** novelty is a specific increment of knowledge. Not "examined for the first time," but what exactly is new and how it differs from predecessors.

**Criteria:**
- Novelty is stated specifically: what exactly is new (typology, model, classification, verification)
- Difference from existing works is demonstrated
- Level of novelty is proportionate to the type of work (master's thesis ≠ doctoral dissertation)

**Novelty scale by type of work:**
- Master's thesis: verification on new data, adaptation of a model, extension of a classification
- PhD (Kandidat) dissertation: new methodology, extension of a model, original typology
- Doctoral dissertation: new concept, theory, fundamental reconceptualization

**Typical errors:**
- Declarative novelty: "For the first time..." without specifying what is new
- Novelty overstated for the level of work
- Novelty does not distinguish the paper from existing research

---

## 9. Theoretical Significance

**Criteria:**
- Specifies what concrete contribution to theory the paper makes (extension of a model, clarification of boundary conditions, operationalization of a concept)
- Significance is connected to the paper's results, not to the importance of the topic
- Significance is not a retelling of the content

**Typical errors:**
- Substituting significance with a retelling of the content
- Significance is not connected to the paper's own results
- Declarative formulations without specifics

---

## 10. Practical Significance

**Criteria:**
- Recipient identified: who can use the results
- Application context identified: where and under what conditions
- Scale of applicability proportionate to the data of the study
- Ideally — confirmed by validation, implementation, or an implementation act

**Typical errors:**
- No recipient and no application context
- Overstated practical significance (results for everyone, while sample is local)

---

## 11. Propositions Submitted for Defense

**Criteria:**
- Contains the author's own result, not a generally known fact
- Is a claim that can be justified by the paper's data
- Is not a retelling of an objective or method
- Contains specifics: numbers, comparisons, conditions

**Typical errors:**
- Proposition is a platitude requiring no proof
- Proposition is a retelling of an objective or method
- Proposition does not contain the author's own result

---

## 12. Conclusions and Conclusion Chapter

**Criteria for conclusions:**
- Bijective correspondence: each objective → one conclusion
- Justification: conclusions are based on data obtained in the study
- No generalization beyond the sample's capacity
- Practical applicability: the area of application of results is specified

**Criteria for the conclusion chapter:**
- Synthesis of results, not retelling of chapters
- Limitations of the study are stated
- Directions for further research are formulated (specific, not "the topic requires further study")

**Typical errors:**
- Conclusion is a platitude not grounded in data
- Generalization beyond the data (12 companies → "all companies in the country")
- Conclusions do not correspond to objectives
- Conclusion chapter is a retelling of chapters
- No directions for further research

---

## 13. Definitions and Terminology

**Criteria:**
- Key terms are defined at first use
- Definition is not a tautology
- If multiple definitions exist — the choice of one is justified
- Term is used consistently throughout the text

**Typical errors:**
- Tautological definition
- Contradictory use of a term in different parts of the paper
- Author's definition without justification for the choice

---

## 14. Argumentation and Logical Errors

**Common logical errors in academic papers:**
- Appeal to authority without critical analysis — citing an author as an argument, without assessing applicability
- Post hoc ergo propter hoc — "after, therefore because of," without isolating the effect
- False dichotomy — "either A or B," when options C, D, E exist
- Circular reasoning — a thesis is proved by itself
- Substitution of correlation for causation — r≠0 interpreted as a causal relationship

**Criterion for correct argumentation:**
- Every thesis is supported by data, a source, or a logical inference
- Alternative explanations are considered
- Limitations of the argumentation method used are identified

---

## 15. Formulations of the Methodological Apparatus in the Introduction

**Mandatory elements of the introduction:**
relevance, problem, state of research on the problem, goal, objectives, object, subject, methods, novelty, theoretical and practical significance, structure of the work.

**Criteria:**
- Theoretical framework: each cited author/theory is tied to a specific element of the study
- Structure of the work: not a formal enumeration (introduction, 3 chapters, conclusion), but a substantive description of the logic
- Methods: not an abstract list of general scientific methods, but a specification of which method is used to address which objective
